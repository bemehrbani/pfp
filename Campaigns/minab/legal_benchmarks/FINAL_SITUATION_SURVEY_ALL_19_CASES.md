## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This comprehensive survey was commissioned by People for Peace & Justice ry (PFPJ ry) to establish the definitive, authoritative judicial and investigative status of all nineteen (19) international benchmark cases concerning military targeting errors, precision standoff strikes, and catastrophic civilian casualty incidents. Spanning four decades of contemporary warfare—from Operation Desert Storm (1991), the NATO air campaign in the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (1999), and the Second Chechen War (1999–2000), through the post-9/11 operations in Iraq, Afghanistan, and Syria, to the Saudi-led Coalition campaign in Yemen (2015–2018) and the Russian invasion of Ukraine (2022)—these nineteen cases form the canonical jurisprudence governing the principles of Distinction, Proportionality, Precautions in Attack, and the Autonomous Procedural Duty to Investigate under International Humanitarian Law (IHL) and International Human Rights Law (IHRL).

### Five Core Comparative Determinations

#### 1. Was there a court proceeding started for them in international or national courts?
Court proceedings were initiated in only 6 of the 19 benchmark cases before international judicial bodies (ECtHR Grand Chamber and Chamber judgments in Hanan v. Germany, Isayeva v. Russia, Banković v. Belgium, and Isayeva, Yusupova and Bazayeva v. Russia; formal review by the ICTY Office of the Prosecutor in Belgrade Embassy, RTS, and Koriša; and formal investigations/arrest warrants by the International Criminal Court in Chernihiv/Ukraine and Afghanistan). In national courts, formal criminal proceedings occurred in only 3 instances (German Federal Prosecutor General in Kunduz, Russian Achkhoy-Martan Military Prosecutor in Katyr-Yurt/Shaami-Yurt, and Ukrainian Prosecutor General in Chernihiv). Domestic civil litigation was overwhelmingly dismissed under sovereign immunity, the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), or the political question doctrine (e.g. Belhas v. Ya'alon in US courts, BGH Amtshaftung dismissals in Germany).

#### 2. Is there any international or national authority investigating the incident?
All 19 cases have been subjected to investigation by authoritative international bodies (UN Independent Commissions of Inquiry, UNAMA Human Rights Units, UN Group of Eminent Experts, UN Secretary-General Boards of Inquiry, Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, Bellingcat, and the ICRC) and/or official national authorities (US CENTCOM AR 15-6 boards, US Air Force Inspector General, US DoD Title V Congressional Reviews, German Bundestag Parliamentary Inquiry Committees, Israeli IDF Chief of Staff Inquiries, Saudi Joint Incident Assessment Team - JIAT, and the Ukrainian Prosecutor General's Office).

#### 3. Is there any ongoing investigation for any of them today (as of late 2026)?
Only Case 17 (Chernihiv Schools Nos. 18 & 21 / Russian Invasion of Ukraine) maintains an ACTIVE, ongoing investigation before the International Criminal Court (ICC OTP) and domestic Ukrainian courts (Prosecutor General's Office - PGO). Cases 13 and 16 (Syria) remain subject to active evidence archiving and structural war crimes investigations under Universal Jurisdiction in European jurisdictions (via IIIM and German/French specialized war crimes units). The remaining 16 cases are officially CLOSED, terminated, or deadlocked: national military investigations were concluded within months; ECtHR judgments reached final non-execution stalemates following Russia's exit from the Council of Europe in 2022; and UN investigative mandates in Yemen (UN GEE) were prematurely extinguished by diplomatic lobbying in October 2021.

#### 4. What is the overarching pattern of command accountability and criminal prosecution?
A profound 'Impunity Chasm' characterizes state practice. Across all 19 cases—responsible for over 1,400 certified civilian deaths, hundreds of maimed children, and the pulverization of schools, hospitals, and bomb shelters—NOT A SINGLE military commander, target planner, or aircrew member has ever been convicted of a criminal offense or court-martialed in national or international courts. The sole criminal conviction in history was of a civilian television director (Dragoljub Milanović of RTS Belgrade) convicted by domestic Serbian courts for failing to evacuate his staff. Military self-investigations (CENTCOM AR 15-6, Air Force IG, Saudi JIAT, IDF Klifi Inquiry) consistently characterize mass civilian slaughter as an 'unfortunate, preventable error' or 'honest mistake' lacking criminal intent (mens rea), frequently promoting responsible commanders (e.g. German Colonel Klein promoted to Brigadier General; Russian General Shamanov decorated as Hero of Russia).

#### 5. What is the record of sovereign compensation and victim restitution?
Sovereign compensation occurs almost exclusively when powerful geopolitical interests collide, rather than from human rights considerations. The United States paid $28 million to China and $4.5 million in ex gratia payments to victims' families for the Belgrade Embassy bombing under a bilateral treaty. In contrast, Iraqi victims of Al-Amiriyah and Dora Farms, and Syrian victims of Al-Jina, received zero compensation. Germany provided voluntary ex gratia humanitarian payments of $5,000 per deceased victim ($430,000 total) in Kunduz while successfully defending against civil liability before the Federal Court of Justice (BGH). Russia paid modest ECtHR moral damages awards (€12,000–€300,000) prior to 2022 while maintaining total criminal impunity. Israel and the Saudi Coalition systematically rejected mandatory restitution ordered by international bodies (such as UNGA Res 51/233 requiring Israel to pay $1.7M for Qana damages).

### Case 01: Bombing of the Chinese Embassy in Belgrade (May 7, 1999, at 23:50 CEST)
* **Locus:** Ulica Trešnjinog Cveta 3, New Belgrade, Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (44.8256° N, 20.4206° E)
* **Belligerent Actor(s):** United States / NATO Operation Allied Force vs. Federal Republic of Yugoslavia / People's Republic of China
* **Weaponry & Munitions:** Three to five 2,000-lb JDAM GPS precision-guided bombs delivered by a USAF B-2 Spirit stealth bomber
* **Intended vs. Actual Target:** Intended target was the Yugoslav Federal Directorate for Supply and Procurement (FDSP, Yugoimport) located at Bulevar Umetnosti 2. Actual physical structure struck was the Embassy of the People's Republic of China, 350 meters away.
* **Certified Casualties:** 3 Chinese journalists killed (Shao Yunhuan of Xinhua; Xu Xinghu and Zhu Ying of Guangming Daily); 20 embassy personnel severely injured; complete structural destruction of the embassy compound and diplomatic residences.
* **Targeting Failure Mechanics:** A classic three-stage intelligence and cartographic breakdown: (1) CIA targeting officer employed navigational 'intersection and resection' techniques derived from artillery plotting to derive coordinates for an address, generating incorrect coordinates; (2) Planners relied upon three outdated peacetime maps (1989 and 1996 Belgrade city maps and a 1997 US National Imagery and Mapping Agency - NIMA map) which did not show the embassy's relocation in 1996; (3) Circular validation: reviews within EUCOM and the Joint Staff merely checked that the coordinates on the targeting data sheet matched the NIMA database, never checking external ground reality or the Yugoslav phone directory; (4) A late warning raised by a mid-level intelligence officer was suppressed by bureaucratic delays.
* **International & Domestic Court Proceedings:** {'international': "ICTY Office of the Prosecutor (OTP): ICTY Prosecutor Carla Del Ponte established an internal committee to review NATO's bombing campaign. In its Final Report (June 2000, paras 80–85), the OTP concluded that the strike resulted from intelligence failure and cartographic negligence rather than intentional targeting, deciding not to open a formal criminal investigation into NATO/US personnel. At the International Court of Justice (ICJ), Yugoslavia initiated the Legality of Use of Force cases against NATO states; however, proceedings against the United States were dismissed for lack of jurisdiction on June 2, 1999.", 'national': 'No domestic criminal proceedings were initiated in the United States or Yugoslavia against military planners. Domestic civil litigation in US federal courts was precluded by sovereign immunity and the political question doctrine.'}

### Case 02: Kunduz River Sandbank Fuel Tankers Airstrike (September 4, 2009, at 01:49 local time (UTC+4:30))
* **Locus:** Sandbank in the Kunduz River, near Omar Khel village, 7 km SW of Kunduz PRT Base, Afghanistan (36.6667° N, 68.8667° E)
* **Belligerent Actor(s):** German Bundeswehr (ISAF Regional Command North) & USAF Strike Eagles vs. Taliban Insurgents / Afghan Civilians
* **Weaponry & Munitions:** Two 500-lb GBU-38 JDAM GPS precision-guided bombs dropped by two USAF F-15E Strike Eagle aircraft
* **Intended vs. Actual Target:** Intended target was an assembly of armed Taliban insurgents mobilizing two hijacked fuel tankers to attack the Kunduz base. Actual object struck was a dense crowd of local villagers and children collecting fuel with buckets and canisters from the immobilized trucks.
* **Certified Casualties:** Between 91 and 142 civilians certified killed (including dozens of young boys; Germany settled ex gratia claims for 91 deceased individuals); 11 wounded; catastrophic blast/thermal incineration of the riverbank.
* **Targeting Failure Mechanics:** Severe cognitive anchoring and confirmation bias: (1) Col. Georg Klein relied exclusively on a single Afghan human informant ('Peter') who claimed that all civilians had cleared the sandbank and only insurgents were present; (2) Infrared FLIR feeds from a B-1 bomber and F-15s showed thermal hot-spots moving around the trucks, which Klein interpreted as combatants; (3) Manufactured 'Troops in Contact' (TIC): Klein declared TIC to secure air support although German forces were safely inside their base 7 km away; (4) Rejection of Pilot Precaution: F-15 pilots repeatedly suggested making a low-altitude 'show of force' pass to disperse the crowd, but Klein refused, fearing insurgents would scatter, ordering immediate weapon release.

## CROSS-CUTTING STRUCTURAL & JURISPRUDENTIAL ANALYSIS

### 1. The Structural Bifurcation: International vs. National Court Trajectories

A comparative review of the 19 benchmark cases reveals a stark divergence between international judicial oversight and domestic judicial proceedings. International tribunals—most notably the European Court of Human Rights (Grand Chamber and Chamber in Hanan, Isayeva, and Bazayeva)—have established rigorous, progressive jurisprudence holding states accountable for upstream planning negligence, institutional information siloing, and weapons disproportionality under the Right to Life (ECHR Article 2). In contrast, national court systems in belligerent states have functioned almost exclusively as protective shields for their military forces. In the United States, civil claims by foreign victims are universally dismissed at the threshold under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA, e.g., Belhas v. Ya'alon), the political question doctrine, or the combatant activities exception of the Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA). In Germany, the Federal Court of Justice (BGH) and Federal Constitutional Court (BVerfG) definitively ruled in the Kunduz litigation that individual victims of international armed conflicts possess no direct private right of action for state liability (Amtshaftung) under customary international law. In Russia, military prosecutors repeatedly dismissed criminal files on Katyr-Yurt and Shaami-Yurt to protect decorated generals. Domestic courts have proven structurally incapable of prosecuting high-ranking military commanders for targeting errors, leaving international tribunals and European Universal Jurisdiction as the sole viable avenues of legal redress.

### 2. The Anatomy of Military Self-Investigations: The Whitewash Architecture

Across the surveyed cases, official military investigations (such as USCENTCOM AR 15-6 boards, the US Air Force Inspector General, Israeli IDF Chief of Staff inquiries, and the Saudi-led Coalition JIAT) exhibit an identical institutional pathology: they concede factual errors while systematically denying criminal culpability. Internal inquiries frequently admit operational breakdowns—such as shift-handover miscommunications in Al-Jina (Case 04), cognitive confirmation bias in Kabul (Case 05), or delays violating Rules of Engagement in Dahyan (Case 15). However, military investigators invariably conclude that the attack was an 'honest mistake' or 'unfortunate, preventable error' committed in good faith, thereby finding no criminal intent (mens rea) or law-of-war violation. This administrative laundering exonerates commanders and operators from disciplinary action or court-martial, perpetuating a culture of impunity where responsible officers are routinely promoted rather than punished (e.g., Col. Klein promoted to Brigadier General after Kunduz; Gen. Shamanov awarded Hero of Russia after Katyr-Yurt).

### 3. Current Investigative Landscape as of Late 2026: Active vs. Extinguished Inquiries

As of late 2026, the nineteen benchmark cases divide into three distinct operational categories: (A) Active & Ongoing Inquiries: Case 17 (Chernihiv Schools / Russian Invasion of Ukraine) is the sole case actively investigated and prosecuted simultaneously before the International Criminal Court (ICC OTP) and domestic national courts (Prosecutor General's Office of Ukraine), backed by active international arrest warrants. Cases 13 and 16 (Syria) remain subject to active structural investigations and evidence archiving under Universal Jurisdiction across specialized war crimes units in Germany, France, and Sweden (in collaboration with the IIIM). (B) Formally Closed Historical Precedents: Cases 01 (Belgrade Embassy), 02 (Kunduz), 04 (Al-Jina), 05 (Kabul), 06 (Dora Farms), 08 (Al-Amiriyah), 09 (Qana 1996), 10 (Qana 2006), 11 (RTS Belgrade), 12 (Koriša), 14 (Abs Hospital), 15 (Dahyan Bus), and 18 (Mastaba Market) are officially closed; internal military boards and international commissions have concluded their mandates, with no domestic or international authority actively pursuing proceedings. (C) Deadlocked / Extinguished Cases: Cases 03 and 19 (Chechnya) reached a permanent enforcement stalemate following Russia's expulsion from the Council of Europe in 2022 and domestic legislation nullifying ECtHR judgments; Case 07 (Dasht-e-Archi) was extinguished by the collapse of the Afghan Republic in August 2021.

### 4. The Reparations Paradigm: Geopolitical Sovereign Power vs. Civilian Vulnerability

State practice regarding financial compensation reveals that restitution under the Chorzów Factory principle (restitutio in integrum / ARSIWA Articles 31–36) is governed by geopolitical power dynamics rather than objective humanitarian harm. In Case 01 (Belgrade Embassy), the United States promptly paid $28 million in property damages and $4.5 million in humanitarian payments to China pursuant to a formal bilateral treaty, driven by the imperative to normalize diplomatic relations with a nuclear peer. Conversely, where civilian victims belonged to non-aligned or politically vulnerable populations in the Global South (Al-Amiriyah, Dora Farms, Al-Jina, Mastaba Market), attacking states paid zero compensation, disclaimed legal liability, and offered no formal apology. In Kunduz (Case 02), Germany disbursed modest ex gratia condolence payments ($5,000 per family, totaling $430,000) while vigorously asserting that no legal right to compensation exists under international law. In Katyr-Yurt and Shaami-Yurt, Russia paid court-ordered moral damages (€12,000–€300,000) solely to comply with formal Council of Europe membership requirements prior to 2022, while refusing to reopen domestic criminal files. In Yemen, the Saudi Coalition created an opaque 'Redress Fund' that delivered negligible assistance to victims.

### 5. Universal Jurisdiction and the ECHR Article 2 Procedural Duty as the Forum of Last Resort

Because domestic courts in attacking states routinely block accountability through sovereign immunity, and international tribunals are often constrained by jurisdictional carve-outs or vetoes, European Universal Jurisdiction has emerged as the definitive legal mechanism of last resort. Under the jurisprudence of the ECtHR Grand Chamber in Hanan v. Germany, an autonomous procedural obligation under Article 2 ECHR requires states exercising military command to conduct effective criminal investigations into lethal extraterritorial strikes. Furthermore, under universal jurisdiction legislation codified in Nordic and European legal systems—such as Chapter 11, Section 6 of the Finnish Penal Code (Rikoslaki) and the German Code of Crimes against International Law (VStGB)—national prosecutors possess extraterritorial competence to investigate war crimes committed abroad. This mechanism provides independent legal NGOs and victim groups with a binding legal basis to submit criminal complaints against foreign military commanders when domestic forums are compromised.
